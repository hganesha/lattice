02The problem

Every agent program stalls in the same place, and it is
never the model.

Pilots work. Production doesn't. The blocker shows up the first time someone from risk, legal, or the data governance team asks four questions about a single agent action — after it has already happened.

Q1

What did it think the question meant?

Intent resolution happens inside a prompt. There is no record of the candidates considered or why one won.

Q2

Which system was it allowed to read?

The agent runs as a service principal with far broader grants than the person who asked.

Q3

Under what policy, on whose authority?

Approval, if it happened, lives in a Slack thread. Purpose was never a field.

Q4

What would have made it stop?

Nothing. The system had no way to say I cannot support this answer.

→A chat transcript is not an answer to any of the four. Neither is a log file.
03Failure modes

Four ways a working demo becomes an incident.

These are not edge cases. They are the default behaviour of an ungoverned agent wired to real systems.

01 — Semantic drift

“Exposure” in the prompt is not “exposure” in the warehouse.

Two definitions coexist. They disagree by a material amount. Nobody notices until the number is in front of a regulator, and finance sides with the warehouse.

02 — Decorative permissions

The caller declares its own entitlements.

If a request body can assert what it is allowed to do, every permission gate in the system is a tautology. Row filters and column masks in Unity Catalog or Fabric never see the person asking.

03 — No floor on confidence

Asked something it cannot support, it answers anyway.

Abstention was never an available output. A weak or stale answer is indistinguishable, in shape and in tone, from a well-evidenced one.

04 — Unreconstructable audit

Explaining the action means re-reading prompts.

There is no artifact that pins what the system meant, what it read, which policy version applied, and who authorised it. Six months later, nobody can produce one.

→Each of these is a governance failure, not an accuracy failure. Better models do not fix any of them.
04The idea

A question does not
return an answer.
It returns a decision.

Lattice compiles a natural-language question against a published Context Contract — a versioned package of meaning, bindings, evidence, policy, and permitted operations for one bounded workflow.

Compilation is deterministic and produces exactly one of four outcomes. Three of them are refusals, and each refusal carries a reason code a governance team can act on.

Separation of concern

The compiler is pure. It cannot execute.

It produces an unsigned plan or a non-execution decision, and nothing else. It holds no key, opens no connection, and calls no source system.

Separation of trust

The API is the only thing that signs.

It verifies identity against your issuer, signs the plan, and pins the contract release into it. Execution is a separate, separately authorised call.

Separation of authority

The executor verifies without asking us.

@lattice/plan-verifier checks signature, expiry, subject and release pin from a published JWKS. No call back to Lattice.

→Compilation before execution. Signed plans before tool calls.

06The deployable unit

The artifact you ship is not an ontology. It is a contract.

A graph is a picture of what you mean. A Context Contract is a versioned, digest-addressed package that a runtime can enforce — and that a reviewer can approve, diff, roll back, and retire.

01

Competency questions

The decisions this contract exists to answer, each with an expected answer shape, an owner, and an impact level.

02

Concept scope

A version-pinned selection from the workspace ontology. The contract inherits meaning; it does not fork it.

03

Source bindings

Which system answers which property, at what freshness, under which credential reference. Never a credential.

04

Evidence & provenance

Content-digested records with validity windows, traceable to every object and relationship that depends on them.

05

Guardrail policies

Per risk tier: minimum evidence strength, maximum evidence age, and whether a human must decide.

06

Declared purposes

With obligations, jurisdictions, and retention. A tier can be restricted to a subset. Undeclared purpose fails closed.

07

Assurance tests

Deterministic gates plus a golden-question suite that fails when a question stops routing where its author linked it.

08

Release state

Immutable, SHA-256 addressed, diffable against any other release, restorable, and rollback-controlled with a mandatory rationale.

→Publishing repeats every critical gate server-side. A UI cannot talk its way past a release check.

08The compile path

Seven gates. Every one of them can stop it.

And every stop produces a reason code, not an apology.

GATE 01
Identity
Asymmetric JWT verified against your issuer. Organization membership resolved independently. The body cannot assert who it is.
GATE 02
Intent
Lexical plus optional vector candidates. Risk-tier score, margin, and confirmation thresholds. Semantic-only picks must be confirmed.
GATE 03
Entities
Governed objects resolved through the pinned concept scope. Ambiguity becomes a typed clarification, not a coin flip.
GATE 04
Evidence
Strength and freshness checked against the tier's policy. Weak or stale context abstains rather than answering.
GATE 05
Purpose
The declared purpose must exist in the contract and be permitted at this tier. Undeclared fails closed.
GATE 06
Policy
Permissions derived from verified token scopes; wildcards stripped. High-risk tiers escalate to a named human.
GATE 07
Signing
Contract, semantic, policy, binding, API, and metric versions pinned. Grounding pinned. Subject bound. Short expiry.
Pinned into every plan

The resolver's own reasoning

Model and index digest, candidate scores, the thresholds applied, the winning margin, matched governed questions, and whether a human confirmed the operation.

Pinned into every plan

Grounding: live or simulated

A plan resolved from documented sample payloads says so, inside the signature. Reference mode must be declared in the published release to be permitted at all.

Enforced at execution

One subject, one use

Principal and tenant are signed in. A plan held by anyone else reports as absent, not forbidden. A rejected attempt does not burn the nonce.

11What it deliberately does not do

It does not want to be your second glossary.

A tool that competes with Purview, with Unity Catalog, or with the person who owns the semantic layer gets blocked in review. Three design decisions exist specifically to prevent that.

Your catalog stays authoritative

It does not re-decide what is sensitive.

Purview, Unity Catalog, and Collibra already own classification. Lattice imports their judgement at binding discovery and marks where it came from. A local judgement is only the fallback.

Detail on slide 14.

Your platform keeps enforcing

It does not stand in front of your access controls.

A binding can run the query as the person who asked, so Unity Catalog row filters and Fabric's own security apply because those systems apply them. The contract becomes an additional constraint, never a replacement.

Detail on slide 13.

Your semantic layer stays yours

It does not want to own your metrics.

Two definitions of “exposure” will disagree, and finance will side with the warehouse. The differentiating claim is compilation, evidence, and signed plans — none of which requires owning a metric registry.

Binding metrics by reference is still open work.

→Every one of these is an adoption fight the product would lose. The next three slides are the proof, not the promise.

A binding is a governed claim, not a connection string.

It declares exactly what may be read, from where, at what age, keyed how, and as whom — and it goes through review like any other claim.

A read, not an extract

Bounded before it runs

Every query is wrapped and limited to n+1 rows — enough for a decision, not enough for a bulk export, and one row past the ceiling so a receipt can say honestly whether it saw everything.

Read-only — and its honest limit

One statement, comments stripped, no side effects

Must begin SELECT or WITH. Comments are stripped before matching, so nothing hides behind them. Mutating keywords and the functions that read files or reach the network are refused.

A blocklist is not a SQL parser. Only PostgreSQL gets a real guarantee, from its READ ONLY transaction. The durable fix is a read-only role on your side.

Export safety

The sanitizer is mandatory

Portable JSON keeps approved credential references and strips sample payloads, secret-like fields, URL user information, and sensitive query parameters. RDF/XML and Turtle carry ontology meaning only.

13Identity propagation

Whether the query runs as you or as a service.

This is the difference between “governed” being true and being a claim. One shared credential means the row filters and column masks your team spent years building never see the person asking.

identityMode: SERVICE — the default
01The person asks. Lattice verifies their token.
02The binding resolves one shared service credential.
03The platform sees the service principal.
04Row filters and column masks apply to the service, not the user.
identityMode: DELEGATED
01The person asks. Lattice verifies their token.
02That token is exchanged — Entra on-behalf-of, or Okta RFC 8693.
03The platform sees the person, scoped to that one resource.
04Your controls apply. The contract is an additional constraint.
Per binding

Not a deployment-wide switch

A reference-data lookup can stay SERVICE while a customer-data read is DELEGATED. The decision sits where the risk is.

It fails, it does not fall back

Silence would be the dangerous outcome

A delegated binding with no exchange configured errors. Quietly running as the service principal would apply none of your controls while the receipt claimed the user's entitlements had.

Scoped per platform

A token for one is useless against the other

Databricks and Fabric each have their own resource identifier. A delegated token is no more broadly usable than the query that needs it.

Still open

What we have not proven yet

Verification against a live Entra or Okta tenant, and delegation for Snowflake and BigQuery — each needs its own resource scopes.

14Catalog federation

Your catalog already decided. We just have to listen.

Asking an author to classify a column a second time in the Studio produces a diverging answer and a governance argument Lattice would lose. So classification is imported at discovery — the exact moment that second judgement would otherwise form.

STEP 01
Catalog decides
Three vocabularies map onto one assertion. Nothing downstream knows which catalog spoke.
STEP 02

Fields arrive pre-classified, carrying source, catalog name, locator, and timestamp — so a federated tag is never confused with a local guess.
STEP 03

Regime names normalise, so PII, PHI, PCI, and CPNI mean one thing whichever catalog said them.
STEP 04

Each value is kept, digested, or withheld — VALUE · DIGEST · WITHHELD — proving what was read without copying it.
Conservative by construction

An unrecognised label becomes CONFIDENTIAL, not INTERNAL — a catalog bothered to label the column, so treating an unfamiliar label as ordinary is the wrong way to be wrong. An unclassified property defaults to INTERNAL, never PUBLIC.

Degrades honestly

A catalog that cannot be read leaves fields unlabelled rather than blocking discovery — and is never reported as “nothing sensitive here”. Reads are bounded by a timeout and refuse redirects.

Still open

Dataplex, Alation, and Atlan adapters. Glossary term import with sameAs mappings. SKOS and DCAT export back to the catalog. OpenLineage events, so your lineage tools show which tables an agent decision consumed. Ownership resolved to a directory principal so approvals can route.

15Governance primitives

Six things that are very hard to retrofit.

Each of these had to be designed into the type system and the storage layer. None of them can be added later as middleware.

01

Separation of duties

The principal who submits a review cannot decide it. The person who requests a runtime approval cannot grant it, and cannot resume their own plan. Enforced in the store, so no interface can route around it.

02

Hash-chained ledgers

Assurance runs and execution receipts chain to their predecessor, and the store refuses to load when the chain does not verify. Verification recomputes each digest from content, so an edited field with a stale digest is caught.

03

Purpose limitation

Contracts declare purposes with obligations, jurisdictions, and retention. A policy can restrict a risk tier to a subset. The compiler fails closed on an undeclared or unpermitted purpose, and pins the declared one into the plan and the receipt.

04

Classification-aware receipts

Governed values above a threshold are withheld from the audit artifact rather than copied into it — enforced at the one place data would otherwise leak into permanent storage. Receipts record how many values were withheld.

05

Grounding pinned into the signature

A plan resolved from documented samples rather than live reads carries grounding: SIMULATED inside the signed payload. The MCP surface warns on it. Reference mode must be declared in the published release.

06

Release control

Immutable digest-backed releases, a digest-backed diff artifact between any two, restore-as-draft that never moves the live pointer, runtime suspension, and rollback that appends an actor-attributed event with a mandatory rationale.

→Risk-tiered intent gates that pin their own scores, thresholds, and margin into the plan are a primitive almost nothing else ships.

17Worked example

Customer proprietary network information.

47 CFR § 64.2005

A rule that distinguishes three uses of the same data. A contract that encodes exactly that distinction, and a compiler that fails closed on the third.

Step 01 — the request

An agent asks in the customer's own words and names a purpose. It cannot invent one — the permitted purposes are discoverable through the MCP surface.

Step 02 — purpose check

The contract declares the three uses the rule distinguishes and permits only two at its operational tier. The third is refused before any source is touched.

Step 03 — evidence & freshness

Policy requires STRONG evidence within a declared maximum age. Stale context abstains rather than answering with a caveat.

Step 04 — human authority

An OPERATIONAL_ACTION escalates. A different named principal decides, with a rationale. The agent cannot approve itself.

Step 05 — the plan

Signed. Pinned to the contract, semantic, policy, binding, API, and metric versions. Bound to one principal and tenant. Short expiry. Single use.

Step 06 — execution

Permissions derive from the executor's verified token scopes, wildcards stripped. A body that asserts its own entitlements is rejected outright.

Step 07 — the receipt

Chained to its predecessor. Every mapped value records whether it was kept, digested, or withheld — and why.

What a regulator gets

One digest-addressed artifact naming the purpose, the policy version, the evidence, the approver, and the exact release it compiled against.

18Usage

Eight surfaces. One person can hold the whole loop.

The Studio is a working environment, not a diagram tool. Everything below writes to a governed draft that a release gate later has to accept.

Model

Ontology canvas

Drag types, draw relationships between node handles, edit properties in the inspector. Lane layout and semantic zones instead of a force-directed hairball.

Ingest

Import Studio

OpenAPI, JSON Schema, RDF/XML, Turtle, CSV. Every collision resolved as merge, separate, or skip. Checksum-stamped, and a proposal until you apply it.

Bind

Source bindings

Live metadata discovery on Databricks, Fabric, and PostgreSQL. Suggested property mappings, declared freshness, read-only scope, credential reference.

Constrain

Policy Studio

One guardrail profile per risk tier. Recommended baselines for uncovered tiers. Editing resets to draft and requires a fresh approval.

Approve

Review Queue

Types, bindings, and policies enter review as claims. Approve, approve-with-exception, or reject — each with a mandatory rationale, each immutable.

Prove

Assurance Studio

Structural, question, mapping, policy, and release gates, plus golden-question routing. Digest-backed artifacts. Critical failures block publish.

Trace

Evidence Registry

Provenance as a dependency graph. Answers “what becomes untrustworthy if this changes?” from the contract itself.

Ship

Release Management

Immutable history, downloadable diffs, semantic-version suggestions, runtime suspension, restore-as-draft, and rationale-backed rollback.

→WCAG AA contrast, a 12px type floor with a large-text option, English and Spanish catalogues, and a pseudo-locale for finding untranslated strings — shipped, not planned.

  • Permissions derived from verified token scopes, wildcards stripped, body-asserted entitlements rejected
  • Plans bound to one subject and tenant; a rejected attempt does not consume the nonce
  • KMS signing via Azure Key Vault and AWS KMS, RFC 7638 thumbprint key IDs, offline verification from a JWKS
  • Separation of duties on reviews and approvals; hash-chained assurance and receipt ledgers
  • Purpose limitation as policy input, plan pin, and audit field
  • Classification federated from Purview, Unity Catalog, and Collibra into classification-aware receipts

  • Delegated identity is covered by tests, not yet by a production Entra or Okta tenant

21Design partnership

Twelve weeks. One decision. One defensible artifact.

Not a platform rollout. Pick a single governed decision your team already makes by hand and put the whole loop around it.

PHASE 01 · WEEKS 1–3

Model one decision

Author the contract against your own vocabulary. Import an existing schema or standard rather than starting blank. Competency questions first; the ontology follows from them. Ends with a published release and a passing assurance run against fixtures.

PHASE 02 · WEEKS 4–8

Bind it to live systems

Delegated identity against your platform, so your row filters and column masks apply. Classification pulled from your catalog at discovery. Policy profiles reviewed and approved by the people who would have to defend them.

PHASE 03 · WEEKS 9–12

Put an agent on it

MCP into whichever runtime you already standardised on. Your executor verifies signed plans offline. Compile traces land in your observability stack next to the rest of the agent trace.

What we need from you

Four things, and a reviewer who cares

One governed decision with real consequences. One data platform. One identity provider. One person with the authority to approve a policy — because a governance product with no governor proves nothing.

What you keep

A contract you own, in formats we do not control

Portable JSON with governed bindings. OWL as RDF/XML and Turtle with stable IRIs. A digest-addressed release history. And a defensible answer to the question every agent program eventually gets asked.
